## 1. The problem this stage solves

By the time a request reaches Stage 4, the earlier stages have already decided **which sites
may receive chargers** (the rule engine's accept / conditional / reject / defer outcomes, §8.1b)
and **how much charging demand each site has** (arrival rates per hour of the week, §8.1a).
The planner then asks one question: *"Place N new charging points (optionally within a budget)
in this planning area, or island-wide. Where do they go, and how many at each site?"*

That is a resource-allocation problem with three properties that rule out ranking sites and
taking the top N:

1. **Returns diminish within a site.** The second charger at a busy car park helps less than
   the first, and the tenth helps very little. Ranking sites once cannot capture that.
2. **Sites have hard capacity.** A site may hold charging points on at most 15% of its car lots
   (`MAX_SHARE_OF_LOTS`, LTA factsheet, 4 Mar 2021), existing and planned points included.
3. **The planner needs an answer when the request cannot be met**, not just "infeasible".

## 2. Objective: drivers rescued per hour

We measure a plan by **how many drivers per hour it rescues**: drivers who would otherwise
arrive to find every charger at a site occupied. Each site is modelled as an Erlang loss system
(M/G/c/c), the standard model for a service with no queue. With `c` chargers, arrival rate `λ`
and mean session `MEAN_SESSION_HOURS` (3 h), the offered load is `a = λ × 3` and the share of
drivers turned away is Erlang B, `B(c, a)`. Adding `x` points rescues

> `λ × (B(c, a) − B(c + x, a))` drivers per hour,

summed over the 48 hour-of-week bins (weekday and weekend hours), weighted `5/7 ÷ 24` and
`2/7 ÷ 24` so that the weights sum to one average hour (§4.4). `c` is the site's **effective**
connectors: those observed today plus any the planner has already committed in this scenario
(`planned_capacity`), so the optimiser never double-counts a planner's own picks.

Erlang B is the same model Stage 2 inverts to *infer* demand from observed occupancy (§8.1a).
Using it forward here means the objective and the demand estimates come from one consistent
model, not two different assumptions.

## 3. The allocator (CP-SAT)

`optev/reasoning/allocate.py`, spec 021, issues #20 and #21.

**Formulation.** For each eligible site `s` and each possible `k`-th new point, a binary
`y[s,k]`; the number of new points is `x[s] = Σ_k y[s,k]`. Erlang B is convex in `c`, so the
marginal gain of the `k`-th point, `g[s,k]`, never increases with `k`. With the ordering
constraint `y[s,k] ≥ y[s,k+1]` the non-linear objective becomes exactly linear:
maximise `Σ g[s,k] × y[s,k]`. The constraints are:

| Constraint | Form |
|---|---|
| Points requested | `Σ x = requested` |
| Budget (if given) | `COST_PER_POINT_SGD × Σ x ≤ budget` |
| Room per site | `x[s] ≤ floor(0.15 × lots) − effective connectors`, floored at 0 |
| Eligibility | only sites the rules accepted or made conditional, inside the area |

OR-Tools CP-SAT solves it with gains scaled ×1000 to integers, a 30 s limit, one worker and a
fixed seed, so **the same request always gives the same plan**. With only a budget, the planner
gets as many points as the budget pays for: `floor(budget / 9,250)`, where SGD 9,250 is the
average installed cost per charger in LTA's ECCG worked examples (tested at 5,500 and 10,500).

**Two decisions worth recording.** A site with only a daily-average estimate (no hourly
profile) uses that rate at weight 1. For a flat profile that gives the same answer, and without
it no-charger sites could not be planned at all. A site's own scenario decision overrides its
baseline decision, so a rule that changes under growth is respected.

## 4. The independent checker

`optev/reasoning/checker.py`, spec 016, issue #22.

An optimiser that proposes a plan should not also be the thing that approves it. The checker
re-tests every constraint **from the design text alone**: a test parses its imports and fails
if it ever imports the allocator. It deliberately repeats the room, budget-floor and
decision-choice arithmetic, because two independent copies are the point. It returns one coded
violation per broken constraint (`room: SYN-S-04 has 6, room 4`) and marks the plan
`checked`. Only checked plans are written or shown.

We tested it by **planting faults** in a correct plan, one per constraint: a rejected site, an
unknown site, a duplicate, a site outside the area, one point short, over budget, over room
and a wrong cost. The checker catches every one, each under its own code, and passes every plan
the allocator produced (12 points island-wide, SGD 300,000, 5 points in Tampines, and 20 points
with SGD 200,000) with zero violations.

## 5. When the request cannot be met

`allocate` returns an `InfeasibilityReport` instead of a plan (spec 022, issue #23):

- **`max_feasible`**: the most points any plan allows under the room and budget limits, found
  with a second solve. This is the planner's actionable number: "ask for at most 32".
- **`binding_constraints`**: each limit (`budget`, `room`) that **on its own** stands in the
  way, meaning that removing it alone would make the request feasible.

Example (sample data): 40 points in Pasir Ris with SGD 300,000 gives `max_feasible` 32 and
`binding_constraints = ["budget"]`. The area has room for 40, but the budget pays for 32. This
equals the answer key built into the synthetic sample, independently of the allocator.

## 6. Scenarios: planning for 2030, not today

`optev/scenario/project.py`, spec 027, issue #28 (projection: Shrey; growth fit: Sushant).

Observed demand is a week in October 2026, but the target is 2030. Each scenario (`low`,
`base`, `high`) multiplies every observed **arrival rate** by a growth multiplier and writes
the result to `scenario_demand`, never to the observed table. We scale rates, never occupancy:
occupancy cannot exceed 100%, so scaling it would hide a surge exactly at the saturated sites
that matter most. A demand estimate that is only a lower bound (a site that was always full)
stays a lower bound. A test confirms that the allocator, given `project()`'s output, produces
exactly the plan it produces from hand-scaled demand.

The multipliers come from a fit to the electric share of new car registrations (data.gov.sg),
projected to Dec 2030 from LTA's current EV fleet: **3.26 / 3.65 / 3.99** for low / base / high
(spec 027 part A, PR #93; these replace the earlier placeholders of 1.4 / 1.8 / 2.2).

## 7. Evaluation

### 7.1 Baselines

Every method answers **the same request with the same eligible sites and the same room**, and
is scored by the same objective (`evaluation/harness.py`, §6). Only the selection logic
differs:

| Baseline | Rule | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Random (mean of 100 seeds) | each point to a random eligible site with room | harness (#39) |
| Population share | split points across areas by residents, then by car lots | harness (#39) |
| **Busiest first** | rank sites by observed utilisation, fill each to its room | Shrey (#36) |
| Spatial spread | each point as far as possible from existing charging | Gurneet (#37) |
| Extend existing | add to sites that already have chargers | Sushant (#38) |

Busiest first is the rule a planner would most plausibly use without a model: "put chargers
where the existing ones are busiest". A site with **no measured utilisation** (no chargers, or
too little data) is ranked after every measured site, never as 0%. Ranking it as 0 would mark
it as a site nobody wants to charge at (§2.3, invariant 2).

### 7.2 Results on the synthetic sample

Base scenario, island-wide unless stated. All methods, all requests: **0 violations**.

| Request | Optimiser (drivers / h) | Best baseline | Uplift | Random (mean) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 points | 1.570 | 1.567 (busiest first) | +0.2% | 1.433 |
| 12 points | 3.743 | 3.658 (busiest first) | +2.3% | 3.398 |
| 30 points | 9.252 | 8.359 (random) | +10.7% | 8.359 |
| 60 points | 18.210 | 16.326 (random) | +11.5% | 16.326 |
| 12 points, Tampines | 3.742 | 3.658 (busiest first) | +2.3% | 3.443 |
| 12 points, Pasir Ris | 3.590 | 3.184 (random) | +12.8% | 3.184 |

Full table for the 12-point request: random 3.398 ± 0.133, population share 3.037, busiest
first 3.658, spatial spread 3.632, extend existing 2.854 (#92), optimiser **3.743**. Extend
existing is the weakest: spreading points thinly over sites that already charge rescues little,
so it is never the best baseline in the table above. The optimiser solves it to proven
optimality in about 15 ms.

**What the table shows.** For a small request, "busiest first" is almost optimal. With five
points, the best move really is to add chargers at the most saturated site. But busiest first
keeps filling that site after the extra points stop helping, so as the request grows it falls
behind even random placement. The optimiser stops adding to a site once the next point would
rescue fewer drivers somewhere else, and its advantage grows with the size of the request:
**from 0.2% at 5 points to over 11% at 30 and 60**. A planner placing hundreds of points
island-wide is in exactly the regime where intuition fails.

## 8. What we tried that did not work

**A minimum number of points per planning area.** The first formulation guaranteed every
planning area a minimum share of new points, a natural-sounding fairness rule. Island-wide,
the area minimums alone added up to about 100 points, so any smaller request was infeasible
before demand was even considered. The number was our own and uncited,
so it was removed (design 1.8): points now go only where they rescue the most drivers, and
an area receiving zero is a valid answer. The unmet-requirement report was simplified to
match (no `affected_areas`).

**Listing "requested" as a binding constraint.** Read literally, "which constraint, if
removed, makes the request feasible" always includes the points target itself, since placing
nothing is always feasible. That tells the planner nothing, so we dropped it, matching the
sample's answer key. The strict "removal alone" rule has a cost we kept rather than hid: a
request that exceeds *both* room and budget lists no single binding constraint, because
neither alone is enough. The report still gives `max_feasible`, and the Allocation page (#87)
explains the case in words.

**Fitting growth to registration counts.** The approved plan fitted an exponential to monthly
EV registrations. It produced 108% growth a year and about 116,000 EVs a month by 2030, in a
market capped by COE quotas at roughly 4,500 new cars a month. The fit was refitted on the
**electric share** of new cars (a logistic curve, bounded by 100%), which gives the 3.3–4.0×
multipliers above.

## 9. Limitations

- **The checker cannot see planned capacity.** `check(allocation, decisions, sites)` receives
  no scenario, so its room uses observed connectors only, and it would pass a plan that
  overfills a site the planner already committed points to. The allocator itself accounts for
  planned capacity correctly; closing the gap needs a design change to the checker's signature.
- **Solver status and optimality gap are logged, not stored.** Neither the `Allocation`
  contract nor the table has a field for them, and the contracts are frozen.
- **Cost per point** comes from grant worked examples for condominium chargers, not a market
  survey. It only matters when a budget is given, and results are reported at both ends of
  its range.
- **Electrical capacity and land access are out of scope.** Recommended sites are planning
  candidates. Conditional sites carry the rules they must still satisfy (e.g. electrical
  capacity, fire safety), shown next to each recommendation on the Allocation page (#87).
